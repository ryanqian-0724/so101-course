1. Diffusion: generating actions out of noise

Recall the averaging trap from chapter 10: when demonstrations go both left and right around an obstacle, a network that predicts the average drives into it. ACT's style input helps. Diffusion Policy (2023, Columbia University and others) solves the problem from a different direction, borrowing the method behind AI image generators.

The training trick is strange at first:

  1. Take a real chunk of actions from a demonstration.
  2. Add a random amount of noise to it, scrambling the numbers.
  3. Train a network to look at the scrambled chunk (and the camera image) and predict the noise that was added.

A network that can spot the noise can remove it. So to act, the robot starts with a chunk of pure random numbers and repeatedly asks the network "what here is noise?", subtracting a little each time. After a number of rounds, a clean, sensible action chunk remains.

Try it: denoise a path

The dots are a planned path, starting as random noise. Step through the denoising and watch a clean path emerge that passes the obstacle on one side. Press "Start from fresh noise" and run it again a few times: sometimes it goes over, sometimes under, but it never takes the crashing "average" route. Different starting noise leads to different, equally valid answers.

Analogy

A sculptor starts with a rough block and removes a little stone at a time until a statue appears. Training taught the sculptor what statues look like. Two different rough blocks give two different statues, both fine, and never a blurry blend of the two.

The price is speed. One decision needs many passes through the network instead of one. The original paper used 100 denoising rounds in training and brought it down to about 10–16 when running on real robots.

Flow matching is a newer relative with the same spirit. The network learns a direction that carries noise toward real actions along a nearly straight route, so it gets there in few steps (commonly 10). Most of the large models below use flow matching to produce their actions.

2. VLAs: robots that start with knowledge of the world

First, a VLM

A vision-language model (VLM) is a large Transformer trained on enormous numbers of images paired with text from the internet. It can look at a photo and answer questions about it. It knows what a cup looks like, what "left of" means, and that bananas are yellow. It has, however, never moved anything.

Then, add actions

A vision-language-action model (VLA) starts from a VLM and trains it further on robot data, so that it outputs actions. Its inputs are camera images, the robot's joint values, and an instruction in ordinary language such as "put the red brick in the box".

There are two ways VLAs produce actions:

  • Actions as words. Chop each joint's range into numbered bins and have the language model "write" the numbers one at a time, as it writes text. Simple, but slow and coarse. Used by early VLAs such as Google's RT-2 (2023) and OpenVLA (2024).
  • A separate action expert. A smaller network attached to the VLM generates a whole chunk of precise actions with flow matching. Used by most current models.

Pretraining and fine-tuning

These models are built in stages. The VLM is pretrained on internet data. The VLA is then pretrained on thousands of hours of demonstrations from many robots. Finally you fine-tune it: continue training briefly on a few dozen demonstrations of your robot and task.

Analogy

ACT is a newborn that must learn everything from your 50 demos. A pretrained VLA is a student who has read millions of illustrated books and watched many other robots work, and now needs a few days of practice on your particular arm.

A common misunderstanding

A pretrained VLA does not work on your SO-101 straight out of the box. Robots differ in shape, cameras and how actions are numbered. The SmolVLA docs state that fine-tuning on your own data is required.

"Size" is the number of parameters. All of these can be trained with LeRobot. The list of supported policies grows quickly: at the time of writing LeRobot included 29.

Does bigger and pretrained actually help? In the SmolVLA paper's own tests on an SO-101 pick-and-place task, SmolVLA scored 90% on seen positions and 50% on unseen ones, against 70% and 40% for ACT. The same paper found pretraining lifted SmolVLA's average on another set of tasks from 51.7% to 78.3%. Treat these as one team's measurements on a few tasks, not a universal ranking.

Try it yourself: fine-tune SmolVLA on your dataset

This is a good second project once ACT works. It needs a GPU with roughly 10–16 GB of memory (or a rented one), and the docs estimate about 4 hours for 20,000 steps on a high-end cloud GPU.

pip install "lerobot[smolvla]"
lerobot-train --policy.path=lerobot/smolvla_base --dataset.repo_id=YOUR_NAME/so101_pick_place --policy.repo_id=YOUR_NAME/smolvla_so101_pick_place --rename_map='{"observation.images.top": "observation.images.camera1", "observation.images.wrist": "observation.images.camera2"}' --batch_size=8 --steps=20000 --output_dir=outputs/train/smolvla_so101 --job_name=smolvla_so101 --policy.device=cuda

Two things differ from ACT. --policy.path=lerobot/smolvla_base starts from the pretrained model instead of random numbers. And --rename_map translates your camera names into the names the pretrained model expects (camera1, camera2); without it training stops with a mismatch error. You must pass the same --rename_map to lerobot-rollout, together with the task sentence, which now matters.

The official example uses a batch size of 64, which needs a very large GPU; 8 is a safer start. In the Windows Miniforge Prompt, single quotes do not work around the rename map; run this one on macOS, Linux or in the cloud.

3. World models: imagining before acting

A policy answers "what should I do?". A world model answers a different question: "what will happen if I do this?" It takes the current situation and a possible action, and predicts the result.

You have one in your head. Before throwing a ball you can picture roughly where it will land. That prediction is not a decision, but it makes good decisions possible.

Try it: plan by imagination

The robot wants to push the block onto the target. Press "Imagine": its world model predicts where the block would end up for seven different pushes, without moving anything. Then "Act on the best one". The real block lands close to, but not exactly on, the prediction, because the model's physics is learned and imperfect.

What world models are used for:

  • Planning. Imagine many options and pick the best, as in the demo.
  • Practising in imagination. Train a policy inside the model's predictions instead of on a real robot. The 2018 paper that popularised the term trained a game-playing agent entirely "inside of its own dream".
  • Making training data. Generate realistic video of situations that would be slow or dangerous to film.
  • Testing policies without risking hardware.

Names you may meet: DreamerV3 (learns games by imagining futures), V-JEPA 2 from Meta (trained on over a million hours of video), Cosmos from NVIDIA, and Genie 3 from Google DeepMind (generates interactive worlds you can move around in for a few minutes).

A recent development blends the two ideas: world action models are trained to predict future video and actions together, on the theory that learning to predict how the world moves teaches useful physics. Several are now available in LeRobot.

Keep expectations honest

Generated video that looks convincing is not the same as correct physics. The companies building these systems say so themselves: objects sometimes change shape or vanish, and whether success inside a world model predicts success in reality is still an open research question.

4. Reinforcement learning: learning by trying

Imitation learning copies a teacher, so it can match you but not beat you, and it never learns from its own mistakes. Reinforcement learning (RL) works differently: the robot tries things, gets a reward (a number that says how well it did), and adjusts to earn more reward.

Imitation learningReinforcement learning
Learns fromHuman demonstrationsIts own attempts and a reward signal
Can exceed the teacher?NoYes
Main difficultyCollecting good demonstrationsNeeds huge numbers of attempts; early attempts are random and can damage a real robot; someone must define and measure "success"
AnalogyLearning to cook by watching videosLearning by cooking and tasting, burning many dinners on the way

Because random flailing is hard on real hardware, practical methods combine both. LeRobot includes one called HIL-SERL ("human in the loop"): it starts from a few demonstrations, lets the robot practise, and has a person take over briefly whenever it goes wrong. Its authors report near-perfect success on tricky tasks after one to two and a half hours of real-world practice. The newest large models are also beginning to add RL on top of imitation, learning from their own experience on the job.

5. Simulation, shared data, and why robots are behind chatbots

Simulation means training in a physics engine, like a video game built for robots. It is fast, safe, and can run thousands of copies at once. Its weakness is the sim-to-real gap: simulated friction, contact, lighting and cameras never match reality exactly, so a policy that is perfect in simulation can stumble on a real table.

Why data is the bottleneck. Language models learned from text that already existed: essentially the written internet. There is no internet of robot movements. Every demonstration needs a physical robot and a person's time, exactly as you experienced in chapter 11.

This is why open projects matter. Every dataset uploaded to the Hub in LeRobot format, yours included, can become training material for the next shared model. Pooling data from different robot bodies is called cross-embodiment training, and it has been shown to help: skills learned on one arm transfer partly to another.

Moravec's paradox

Computers beat world champions at chess decades ago, yet folding a towel is still a research problem. The roboticist Hans Moravec noted in the 1980s that the things humans find hard (logic, arithmetic) are easy for machines, while the things a one-year-old does without thinking (seeing, grasping, balancing) are extremely hard. Our effortless skills are effortless because evolution spent hundreds of millions of years on them. After fighting to make an arm pick up one brick, you understand this better than most people.
