What you are going to do

Your kit contains two small robot arms. By the end of this course, one of them will pick up an object and put it in a box by itself, looking through a camera and deciding how to move. Nobody will have written instructions like "move 12 cm left". Instead, you will show the robot what to do about fifty times, and a computer program will work out the pattern.

This way of teaching is called imitation learning. It is how most robot-learning research is done today, and your kit is the same kind of tool researchers use, just smaller and cheaper.

Analogy

Think about learning to tie shoelaces. Nobody gave you a list of finger coordinates. Someone showed you, many times, and your brain found the pattern. You will do the same for the robot: you are the teacher, the recorded demonstrations are the lessons, and the trained program is what the robot "remembers".

The five stages

  1. Build. You assemble two arms. The leader is the one you hold; the follower is the one that does the work.
  2. Teleoperate. You move the leader by hand, and the follower copies you instantly. This is called teleoperation: operating something from a distance.
  3. Record. While you teleoperate a task, the computer saves everything: what the cameras saw and how every joint moved, 30 times per second. Each attempt is an episode; the pile of episodes is a dataset.
  4. Train. A program studies the dataset and adjusts millions of internal numbers until it can predict "given this camera image and these joint angles, the human moved like this".
  5. Run. The leader is put away. The trained program now watches the camera and moves the follower itself.

The one word to learn today: policy

The thing that training produces has a name: a policy. A policy is a function. It takes in what the robot currently senses (the observation) and gives back what the robot should do next (the action).

When you teleoperate, you are the policy: your eyes are the observation and your hand on the leader arm produces the action. Training builds a program that imitates the policy in your head.

Why not just program the moves?

You could write "go to position A, close gripper, go to position B". That works in a factory where every part arrives in exactly the same place. It breaks the moment the object is 3 cm to the left, rotated, or a different colour. Writing rules for every possible situation is impossible, so instead we let the robot learn from examples that include that variety.

The tools, and who makes them

SO-101

The robot arm design. Open-source, 3D-printed, designed by TheRobotStudio together with Hugging Face. Also sold as "SO-ARM101".

LeRobot

The free software, written in Python by Hugging Face, that talks to the motors, records data, trains policies and runs them.

Hugging Face Hub

A website where people share datasets and trained models. Your recordings and your policy will be stored there.
