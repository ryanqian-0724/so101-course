Chapter 9 · Ideas

How machines learn

"Training a neural network" sounds mysterious. It is one simple idea repeated millions of times: guess, measure how wrong the guess was, adjust slightly. This chapter builds that idea from nothing.

25 minutes2 interactive demosNo robot needed

Learning means finding good numbers

Suppose you want a rule that predicts one number from another, for example how far a joint moves from how long the motor runs. You collect some examples and plot them. The simplest possible "model" is a straight line, y = w · x + b. It has two adjustable numbers, w (the slope) and b (the height). Those adjustable numbers are called parameters.

"Learning" means finding the values of w and b that make the line fit the examples. A computer does it like this:

  1. Start with random parameters. The line is in a silly place.
  2. Measure how wrong it is. For every example, take the gap between the line's prediction and the true answer; combine the gaps into a single number called the loss. Big loss = bad fit.
  3. Work out which way to nudge each parameter to make the loss a little smaller. That direction is called the gradient.
  4. Nudge, and repeat. Each repetition is one training step.

This procedure is gradient descent, and it is how every model in this course is trained, from this two-parameter line up to models with billions of parameters.

Try it: train a model with two parameters

Press "One step" a few times and watch the line swing toward the dots while the loss curve drops. Then reset, set the learning rate above 1.8 and train again: the steps are so big that the line jumps past the best position and the loss grows. Too small, and learning takes forever. Choosing this step size is a large part of the craft of training.

Analogy

You are on a foggy hillside and want to reach the valley. You cannot see far, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, step again. The height is the loss, your position is the parameters, and the length of your stride is the learning rate. You will reach a valley, though not always the deepest one.

From a line to a neural network

A straight line cannot describe "what a camera image of a red cube looks like". For that you need a far more flexible function. A neural network is exactly that: a very large formula made of simple pieces (multiply, add, and a small bend) stacked in layers, with millions of parameters instead of two.

Nobody decides what each parameter should be. They all start random and are nudged by gradient descent, a tiny amount per step, exactly like w and b above. After enough steps the network as a whole computes something useful, even though no single parameter means anything on its own.

Two kinds of network you will hear about

Convolutional network (CNN, e.g. "ResNet"): made for images. It slides small pattern detectors across the picture, finding edges first, then shapes, then object parts. Your policy uses one to turn each camera frame into a compact description.

Transformer: takes a list of items (image pieces, joint readings, words) and, in every layer, lets each item pull in information from the others that are relevant to it. That mechanism is called attention. Transformers power both chatbots and modern robot policies.

A worked example with real numbers from your project: 50 episodes of 30 seconds at 30 frames per second is 50 × 30 × 30 = 45,000 frames. With a batch of 8, one epoch is 45,000 ÷ 8 ≈ 5,600 steps. LeRobot's docs say imitation learning "typically converges within 5–10 epochs", so roughly 28,000 to 56,000 steps.

The real goal: working on things it has not seen

A model that perfectly reproduces its training examples is not necessarily good. What you want is generalisation: doing well on new situations. The opposite failure is overfitting: memorising the examples, including their accidents and noise, without grasping the pattern.

Analogy

Two students prepare for a maths exam from the same ten past papers. One memorises the ten answer sheets. The other learns the methods. On the ten old papers the memoriser scores 100%. On a new paper, only the second student passes. Training loss is the score on the old papers.

This is the single most useful idea for the rest of the course. Your robot will be trained on 50 demonstrations and then must act in a world that is never identical to any of them. Three practical consequences:

  • A low loss does not prove the robot works. The only real test is running it.
  • Variety in the data fights overfitting. If the cube was always in the same spot, the model can "succeed" by memorising one motion and ignoring the camera. Move the cube around and it is forced to look.
  • More training is not always better. Past a certain point the model polishes its memory of the training set at the cost of everything else.
