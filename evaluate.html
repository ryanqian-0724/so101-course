Windows: if loading the policy by its Hub name fails

A known problem on Windows can turn the / in the name into a \ and produce an error mentioning "Repo id must use alphanumeric chars". The workaround is to use a folder on your computer.

If you trained on this computer, the folder already exists:

--policy.path=outputs/train/act_so101_pick_place/checkpoints/last/pretrained_model

If you trained in the cloud, download the policy first, then point to the folder:

hf download YOUR_NAME/act_so101_pick_place --local-dir my_policy
--policy.path=my_policy
Watch what happens

Place the object on one of your five marks and start. The first time the arm reaches out on its own and closes on the object is a moment worth filming. It may also miss, hesitate, or grab air. All of those are information.

Measure it properly

"It sort of works" is not a result. LeRobot has no automatic success detector for real robots, so you count by hand, the way research papers do.

  1. Define success before you start. For example: "the brick is inside the box within 30 seconds, without human help".
  2. Run 10 trials at trained positions (2 at each of your 5 marks). Write down success or failure for each.
  3. Run 10 trials at new positions, between and around the marks.
  4. Report two numbers: success rate on seen positions and on unseen positions.

Why two numbers, and why ten trials?

The gap between seen and unseen positions measures generalisation: did the policy learn to look, or did it memorise five motions? And ten trials because one success proves little: a policy that works 30% of the time will often succeed on the first try by luck. Even ten trials is a rough measurement; 7 out of 10 and 5 out of 10 are not reliably different.

For reference, people training ACT on the SO-101 commonly report something like 40–60% on their first serious attempt, rising substantially once they fix their data. The team behind SmolVLA measured ACT at 70% on seen and 40% on unseen positions for a similar pick-and-place task. A first result in that region is normal, not a failure.

A real example

One builder ran the same trained ACT policy on two computers. On a laptop that managed only about 15 decisions per second it succeeded 4 times out of 10. On a machine with a GPU running at the full 30 per second it succeeded 9 out of 10. Nothing about the policy changed, only how fast the loop ran.

The improvement loop

This is the dashed arrow from chapter 1, and it is how real robot-learning work is done.

  1. Observe a failure pattern (not a single failure: a pattern).
  2. Form a hypothesis using the table above. "It fails on the left because only 5 of my episodes were on the left."
  3. Change one thing. Record 20 more episodes on the left and add them to the dataset.
  4. Retrain and measure again with the same 20 trials.
  5. Compare the numbers. Did the hypothesis hold?

Change one thing at a time. If you move the camera, change the object and add data, you will not know which change helped.

Record the robot's attempts for later review

The episodic strategy runs the policy and saves each attempt as a dataset, which you can then watch in the online viewer to study failures frame by frame. The dataset name must begin with rollout_.

lerobot-rollout --strategy.type=episodic --policy.path=YOUR_NAME/act_so101_pick_place --robot.type=so101_follower --robot.port=COM5 --robot.id=my_follower_arm --robot.cameras="{ top: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 1, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30}, wrist: {type: opencv, index_or_path: 0, width: 640, height: 480, fps: 30} }" --dataset.repo_id=YOUR_NAME/rollout_pick_place --dataset.num_episodes=10 --dataset.episode_time_s=30 --dataset.reset_time_s=10 --dataset.single_task="Pick up the red brick and put it in the box"

Shown on one line with a Windows port; on macOS or Linux change the port. The same arrow keys as in recording end or redo an attempt.

Ideas for your next experiments

  • How little data is enough? Train on only 10, 25 and 50 of your episodes and compare success rates. You will measure for yourself why the docs say 25 was "not enough".
  • Break it on purpose. Move the camera 5 cm, or switch off a lamp, and measure the drop. Then you know how fragile the policy is and to what.
  • Add a distractor. Put a second, different object on the table. Does the policy still find the right one?
  • A second task. Stacking two bricks, pushing an object to a mark, or opening a small drawer.
