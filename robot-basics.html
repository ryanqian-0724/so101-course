How a robot arm works

An arm is six motors in a chain. Understand one motor and how they are chained, and nothing later in the course will feel like magic.

20 minutes3 interactive demos

Joints: the arm is a chain

Your own arm bends at the shoulder, elbow and wrist. Each place it bends is a joint. The SO-101 has five joints plus a gripper, and each one is turned by its own motor. LeRobot gives them these names, and you will see them in every data file:

Motor IDName in LeRobotWhat it doesYour body's version
1shoulder_panSwings the whole arm left and rightTurning at the waist
2shoulder_liftRaises and lowers the upper armShoulder
3elbow_flexBends the elbowElbow
4wrist_flexTilts the hand up and downWrist nodding
5wrist_rollTwists the handTurning a door handle
6gripperOpens and closes the jawsThumb and finger pinching

The number of independent ways a robot can move is its degrees of freedom (DOF). The SO-101 is described as "5+1": five for positioning the hand, one for the gripper.

Try it: move each jointsimplified side view

Move the sliders. Notice that six numbers completely describe the arm's pose. That list of six numbers is what LeRobot calls the robot's state, and it is exactly what gets recorded and what a policy outputs. Also notice that moving the shoulder moves everything after it: joints are chained.

Key idea

To a computer, "where is the arm?" and "where should the arm go?" are both just a list of six numbers. Everything in this course (recording, training, running a policy) is about reading or writing that list.

Inside one motor: the servo

The motors in your kit are not simple spinning motors like in a fan. Each is a servo (model Feetech STS3215): a small box that contains four things working together.

1. Electric motor

Spins very fast but is weak, like a tiny fan motor.

2. Gearbox

Metal gears that trade speed for strength. A ratio of 1/345 means the motor spins 345 times for one turn of the output.

3. Encoder

A magnetic sensor that measures the output angle as a number from 0 to 4095 (4096 steps per full turn, about 0.09° each).

4. Controller chip

A tiny computer that compares "where am I?" with "where was I told to be?" and powers the motor to close the gap.

So when the computer says "joint 3, go to 80°", it does not control electricity directly. It sends a goal, and the servo's own chip does the work of getting there and holding the position, even if something pushes on it.

Why gears?

Power is roughly speed × turning force (torque). A small motor has plenty of speed and little torque. Gears swap one for the other, the same way a low gear on a bicycle lets you climb a hill slowly. The 1/345 gearbox turns a weak, fast spin into a slow, strong one that can lift the arm.

How the servo reaches its target: feedback

The controller chip repeats one simple loop over and over, far faster than you can see: measure the angle, compute the error (target minus actual), push harder the bigger the error is. This is called feedback control. Two settings shape the behaviour:

  • P (proportional) gain: how hard to push per degree of error. Too low and the motor is lazy; too high and it overshoots and shakes.
  • D (derivative) gain: a brake that grows with speed, which calms the overshoot.
Try it: tune a servotarget jumps to 60°, then to 20°

Set P high and D to zero: the motor shoots past the target and wobbles. That is exactly what a "jittery" arm is. Now switch on the weight: with a low P gain the motor sags below its target, because it needs some error before it pushes hard enough to hold the load. LeRobot sets these gains for you, but now you know what is happening when an arm shakes or droops.

Leader and follower

Both arms have the same shape and the same six joints, but they do opposite jobs.

Name in commandsso101_leader (a "teleoperator")so101_follower (a "robot")
Why does the leader have different gears?

Try turning a high-ratio gearbox backwards by hand: it feels stiff, because you are spinning the little motor 345 times per turn. A leader arm like that would be tiring to move. So the leader uses lighter gearing (1/147 and 1/191) on most joints so it moves easily in your hand. The shoulder lift keeps 1/345 because that joint carries the whole arm's weight, and the extra friction stops it from flopping down. In the words of the official docs, the mix lets the leader "both sustain its own weight" and "be moved without requiring much force".

The control loop

Teleoperation is a loop that the computer runs again and again:

  1. Read the six encoder values from the leader.
  2. Send them as goal positions to the six follower servos.
  3. Wait a moment, then repeat.

How often the loop repeats per second is the control frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). LeRobot's default is 30 Hz, the same as the camera's 30 frames per second.

Try it: teleoperation in slow motionsimulation

Drop the loop rate to 2 Hz. The follower now moves in jerks, because it only hears a new goal twice per second (the dashed ghost shows the last goal it was sent). At 30 Hz the steps are too small to see. The follower is also always slightly behind, because a motor cannot move instantly. Keep this picture in mind: when a policy drives the robot later, it replaces the leader in this exact loop.

How the computer talks to six motors

Each arm has one small circuit board, the motor control board (also called a bus servo adapter). It connects to your computer with a USB-C cable and to a power supply with a separate plug. The six servos are not wired to the board individually. They are daisy-chained: board → motor 1 → motor 2 → … → motor 6, each link a short 3-wire cable.

All six motors share those same wires, which is called a bus. To avoid everyone talking at once, each servo has an ID number (1 to 6). The computer says "ID 3, report your angle", and only motor 3 answers.

Analogy

A bus is like a classroom with one teacher. The teacher calls a student by name, and only that student answers. If two students have the same name, both answer at once and nobody understands. That is why chapter 5 exists: brand-new motors all come with ID 1, and you must give each a unique ID before chaining them.

Remember

USB carries only data to the arm. The motors get their power from the separate power supply. Both must be plugged in for anything to move.
